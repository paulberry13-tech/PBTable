Once per installation

Standing the platform up

PBTable

Done once and never again. Each product in the family gets its own database — the tournament software cannot see club nights or league fixtures, and never will.

  • Create the Supabase project, and point this folder's tooling at it.
  • Apply the migrations in order — tables, the rules that guard them, and the views that do the scoring maths.
  • Bootstrap the first organiser by hand. Nobody can make themselves an admin through the app; the first one is inserted directly, and after that admins add admins.
  • Publish three static pages. There is no server to run and nothing to keep patched — the database is the application.
2

Weeks before · organiser admin

Setting up a tournament

Organiser

The organiser creates the tournament, then describes the events they are running. Almost everything downstream — who may enter, how long the day takes, how a match is won — is decided here.

  • The tournament: name, venue, dates, how many courts, and how long a game takes. Those last two drive every time estimate later.
  • The events: men's, women's, mixed, singles or doubles, with an optional band like 3.0–3.5 or 50+.
  • How each event is scored — and this is per stage, not per tournament. A single game to 15 in the pools to keep the day moving, best of three to 11 for the final. Real tournaments do this; most software will not let them.
  • The rules of entry: fee per player, a cap on entries, a rating ceiling, a combined-rating cap for pairs, an age band.
The link is the product. Each tournament gets a readable web address and a QR code to put on a poster. A player who follows it lands straight in the right event and is never asked to pick from a list — which is the one mistake you can never take back.

Getting the entries in

PlayerOrganiser Automatic

Two routes into the same place. The organiser can type entries in themselves, or open the tournament and let players enter. Most will do both — a few names typed in, the rest self-served.

  • The player signs in with a six-digit code emailed to them. No password to forget between tournaments, and their email is already their identity here because it is how partners invite them.
  • One profile, every tournament. Name, gender, date of birth, rating. These decide which events they are allowed to enter, so the profile comes first.
  • They tick every event they want in one go — naming a different partner in each, which is what people actually do — see a running total, and confirm once.
  • The partner gets an invitation and must accept. Until they do, the entry openly reads "needs a partner", and the organiser can see it too.
  • Each player pays their own share. One card payment covers every event they entered.
A change of partner never costs anyone twice. Fees belong to people, not to entries. Swap a partner after both have paid and only the person who left is refunded; the one who stayed keeps their payment.
4

Entries close · organiser admin

Seeding and the draw

OrganiserAutomatic

The organiser seeds the field and generates the draw. From that moment every match of the event exists in the database, empty and waiting.

  • Seeding runs off ratings, and the organiser overrules it. Ratings carry their source — an official 4.0 and a self-assessed 4.0 are different claims, and the organiser chooses which source counts.
  • Pools are snake-seeded so the strong entries spread out, then every pairing in each pool becomes a match.
  • The knockout bracket is built final-first, so that when the first-round matches are created they already know which slot their winner belongs in.
  • Byes resolve themselves, and every game row is created up front — so on the day, scoring is pure fill-in.
5

The night before · organiser admin

Putting matches on courts

OrganiserChecks

The organiser assigns each match a court and a time. The software does not do this for them — deliberately. A real day has constraints no algorithm knows: someone leaving at three, the show court you want the final on, the referee who is only there in the morning.

What it does instead is the arithmetic and the checking.

  • A running day-length estimate while the draw is being set up, which turns red past six hours.
  • Court double-bookings — flagged when two matches overlap, not merely when they start at the same minute.
  • A player on two courts at once, checked across every event in the tournament. This is the one humans reliably get wrong, because the clash is never visible on the grid you happen to be looking at.
6

The day itself · every screen at once

Running the day

OrganiserPlayer Automatic
  • Players check in as they arrive. If check-in is required, the database will refuse to record a score for anyone who has not — a no-show cannot silently become a result.
  • Scores go in from the keyboard: type, press enter, jump to the next. The winner advances the moment the deciding score is saved.
  • Every screen updates itself. The public results page, the player's phone and the court-side display all re-read the same data.
  • The day's delay is measured, not guessed. Each result is timestamped as it is recorded, so the software knows the tournament is running twelve minutes behind and says so — on the big screen and on every player's own schedule.
What a player sees on their phone: "≈ 11:22 (scheduled 11:10) · Court 1 · v Vane & Cadogan". Court reads TBC when it has not been assigned yet, rather than showing a number that will change.

What the software will not let you do

These are enforced in the database itself, not in the pages. That matters: a rule in a web page can be sidestepped by a stale browser tab or a direct API call. A rule in the database holds no matter how the write arrives.

The ruleWhy it exists
A woman cannot be entered in a men's event, and a mixed pair cannot be two men Gender is stored as a full word and checked on every write — a two-format muddle caused live bugs on a sibling product
Nobody enters the same event twice, and a doubles entry never gets a third player Caught even when several rows are inserted in one go
You may play up a band, never down A 4.2 player cannot drop into a 3.5 event — and the same check is applied to their partner
Age counts as of 31 December of the tournament year The pickleball convention: a December fiftieth birthday plays 50+ all season
An entry is not in the draw until the pair is complete and the fees are paid Recalculated automatically, so the competitive state cannot drift from the money
No score is recorded for a player who has not checked in Switchable per tournament, off by default for casual events
A match that has already been played cannot be quietly rewritten Advancement refuses to disturb a scored match; the organiser clears it deliberately
Players cannot check themselves in, or change a roster once the draw exists The organiser can — a day-of substitution is a real thing they must be able to do

Where everything lives

Twelve tables. The shape worth understanding is the middle one: an entry is a player or a pair in one event, and it is the thing that survives everything else. A partner can change, a fee can be refunded, a seed can move — the entry stays, which is why results and brackets can hang off it safely.

GroupHolds
The event Tournaments, their settings, and the events within them — with scoring configured separately for pools, knockout rounds and the final
The people Players in a tournament, their ratings with the source of each, and cross-tournament accounts for those who entered themselves
The entries Entries and who is in them, partner invitations, and one fee per person per entry
The competition Pools, matches — each knowing where its winner goes next — and the individual games within a match
The reckoning Views that compute results, pool standings and clashes. Scoring rules live in one place, so changing them changes them everywhere
